A model, with sliders

The Electricity Question

How big is AI? As important as the internet? On par with electricity? A rounding error, like the blockchain? A lot of conversations circle back to this question. This is an attempt to help ask questions, develop potential answers, and have better conversations on the topic.

A usable model and mental framework, meant to be transparent and straightforward. The core assumptions are built in as sliders, with formulas included at the bottom. You start with a gut assessment, then work through the sub-questions one at a time. As a check, the model reproduces a published estimate.

It's really hard to have conversations about AI, and about this question in particular. People use the same words but arrive at very different answers, often because they're talking about different things.

This breaks the big question into a few smaller ones that are easier to talk about: How much of our work could AI do? How much of that will actually change? How much cheaper does it get? Could it speed up discovery itself? What could go wrong? And what might it mean for people, now and in the future? If two people land in different places, the sliders can help show where.

Now, the numbers

The whole model fits on one line. Each term gets its own step below.

Work AI can do × share that switches × how much cheaper it gets + faster invention − what goes wrong

The first three terms are the standard way economists add up the effect of a new technology: if 5% of the economy's work gets 14% cheaper, the economy gets about 5% × 14% ≈ 0.7% more productive. Those are roughly Daron Acemoglu's numbers, which point to a modest effect. People disagree about whether those numbers are too low, about right or too high, and whether something is missing.

What your answers suggest

A plain-language summary of everything above. It updates as you move the sliders, and it's what "Copy my answers" copies.

    How the scale is calibrated

    The scale is Silver's. Turning GDP into a rating is our addition, and it works like the earthquake scale: rating = 8 + log10(size ÷ internet). With the internet at 10% of GDP, that puts 1% at 7, 10% at 8, and 100% (a doubling of living standards) at 9. Silver puts electricity and the internet both at 8. This mapping spreads them out, so electricity lands around 8.5.

    The benchmark sizes are back-of-envelope: peak contribution to productivity growth × years at that pace. Crafts finds that steam never added more than about 0.4 points a year to British productivity growth, while electricity and computing each added closer to 1 point a year at their peaks. Change them if you read the history differently; everything re-scores.

    Checks: does it reproduce known results?

    As a check, the model should match a published estimate when given the same inputs. With Acemoglu's inputs (20% of tasks exposed, 23% of those profitable to automate, 14.4% cost saving), it should reproduce his estimate.

    The small gap on productivity comes from compounding: we use the exact log form of Hulten's rule, which is slightly larger than Acemoglu's linear approximation. Published forecasts cover different horizons and methods (Goldman is global, over 10 years; Penn Wharton runs to 2055), so treat their placement on the scale as a rough guide.

    The math, in full

    Everything is the change in GDP relative to a world without AI. Time t runs from 0 to 25 years.

    Adoption. An S-curve: A(t) = ceiling / (1 + exp(−k(t − T))), where T is the years-to-halfway slider and k = 2·ln 9 / T, so adoption goes from 10% to 90% of its ceiling between T/2 and 1.5T.

    Productivity from doing existing work. Hulten's theorem: the log productivity gain is the share of the economy affected times the log productivity gain on it: exposure × A(t) × ln(1 / (1 − saving)). For small savings, ln(1/(1−s)) ≈ s, which gives the familiar "share × saving." This is exact if the economy combines tasks with a unit elasticity of substitution (Cobb-Douglas).

    Faster invention. Adds science × A(t)/ceiling to the productivity growth rate each year, so it accumulates: Σ science × A(τ)/ceiling.

    Investment follows. When productivity rises, firms invest until the capital stock catches up, which raises output by a further factor of 1 / (1 − α), with capital share α = 0.35. So ln GDP gain = (task gain + invention gain) / 0.65. Acemoglu's own step from productivity to GDP implies a similar factor.

    Incidents. Each year, a major incident happens with probability p × m(t), knocks severity off GDP, and fades with a five-year half-life. Losses stack.

    Catastrophe. A constant annual hazard h set so that 1 − (1 − h)^25 equals your 25-year probability, scaled each year by m(t). If it happens, GDP is permanently −severity relative to a world without AI, replacing any gains.

    Risk and footprint. m(t) = (1 − link) + link × F(t) / 20%, where F(t) = exposure × A(t) is the share of the economy's work AI is doing. At link 0 your probabilities apply as entered. At 100%, they're the probabilities for an AI doing a fifth of the economy's work, and scale in proportion to how much it actually does.

    Uncertainty. Each run multiplies exposure, saving, years-to-halfway and science by independent log-normal noise with spread σ = 0.7 × uncertainty. The same 2,000 random draws are reused every time, so moving a slider changes the answer, not the dice.

    Is it worth it. Each run's gain is weighted: gains captured by a concentrated group count at (1 − discount), so a gain of x counts as x × (1 − concentration × discount). Losses count in full. Then a certainty equivalent with constant relative risk aversion η (the "bad outcomes" slider): CE = (mean[(1 + x)^(1−η)])^(1/(1−η)) − 1, which at η = 1 is exp(mean[ln(1 + x)]) − 1. At η = 0 it's the plain average.

    What it leaves out.

    • Bottlenecks. Unit elasticity means cheaper tasks don't shrink as a share of the economy. If tasks are strong complements, the things AI can't do become the bottleneck (Baumol's cost disease), and large savings matter less. This could make the higher estimates too high.
    • Who wins. Wages, prices and inequality are not modeled, except through the concentration sliders.
    • New products. The model counts existing work getting cheaper, plus faster invention, but not entirely new goods and services. This could make the lower estimates too low.
    • Costs. Energy, chips and the investment itself are treated as paid for out of the gains.
    • The long run. Nothing after 25 years. Slow scenarios are not finished by then.
